# Example 3 — Textual question: why is "I don't know" a virtue?

**Question.** On what basis does this method treat "I don't know" as a correct answer rather than a failure?

**Sources.**
1. Babylonian Talmud, *Berakhot* 4a: "Teach your tongue to say 'I do not know.'" → **Certain** (explicit).
2. The Talmud regularly ends a discussion with *Teiku*, leaving a question unresolved instead of forcing an answer → **Certain** (a common, observable usage).
3. *Pirkei Avot* 5:7 (5:9 in some editions) lists among the traits of a wise person that, about what he has not heard, he says "I have not heard" → **Certain** (explicit).

**Reasoning.**
- From sources 1 and 3: admitting ignorance is presented as a trait of the wise → **Certain**.
- From source 2, rule *binyan av* (a general principle drawn from many cases): the tradition accepts an unresolved conclusion as a legitimate result of study → **Strong**.

**Counter-check.** Objection: "A scholar who often says 'I don't know' is useless; people need answers." Response: the sources do not praise ignorance itself, only honesty about it. Saying "I don't know" together with what would resolve the question is still useful. An invented answer misleads.

**Conclusion.** **Strong.** For an AI agent, the practical rule follows: an honest *Teiku* is better than a confident fabrication.
