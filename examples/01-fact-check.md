# Example 1 — Fact-checking: "We only use 10% of our brains"

**Question.** Is it true that humans use only 10% of their brains? Define "use": whether large parts of the brain are permanently inactive or unnecessary.

**Sources.**
1. Functional brain imaging (fMRI, PET) studies, as summarized in standard neuroscience textbooks, show activity across virtually all brain regions over the course of a day.
2. Clinical neurology: damage to almost any brain region causes some measurable deficit.
3. Energy budget: the brain is about 2% of body mass but uses roughly 20% of the body's resting energy (standard physiology references).

**Reasoning.**
- Imaging shows activity in all regions over time → source 1 → **Certain** (well-replicated).
- If 90% were unused, damage there would usually cause no deficit. Clinically it does → source 2, rule: inference from consequences → **Strong**.
- Evolution is unlikely to keep an organ that is 90% idle yet so costly → source 3, rule: analogy from biological economy → **Probable** only, because this is an argument from analogy, not direct evidence.

**Counter-check.** The strongest version of the claim: "At any single moment, only a minority of neurons fire." That is roughly true: activity is sparse at any instant. But it does not mean 90% of the brain is unused, just as not every piano key sounds at once. The claim is popular because it mixes up the two senses of "use" defined above.

**Conclusion.** The claim is false in the sense people usually mean → **Strong**. Where it comes from historically is **Unknown** to me with confidence; several origins are suggested, and I won't pick one without a verified source.
